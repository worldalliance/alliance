# Provenance System: Problem Statement and Design Considerations

I want agents working on my repo to distinguish between two broad kinds of information:

- **Human information:** things users explicitly said, requested, constrained, selected, or otherwise meaningfully decided.
- **Agent information:** things agents proposed, inferred, assumed, or decided while reasoning about or implementing the system.

The main goal is to prevent future agents from treating existing code, architecture, or prior agent decisions as authoritative merely because they already exist.

Most, if not nearly all, of the code in this repo will be written by agents. Human intent may exist upstream of that code, but humans generally will not manually maintain provenance documentation. Existing implementation therefore proves only that some behavior existed; it does not by itself prove that a human wanted that behavior.

This is especially important for legacy code created before the provenance system exists. Its origin may be human, agent, mixed, or unknown, so future agents should avoid assuming either human intent or agent intent without evidence.

## Human intent, delegation, and approval

Human-authored information should not be independently changed by agents, although agents may maintain its representation: they can preserve human statements, quote or summarize them, reorganize provenance records, and update agent-created interpretations when appropriate.

A critical distinction is that human involvement is not the same as human origin.

For example:

> “Do whatever you prescribe.”

or:

> “Go with your recommendation.”

should not automatically make the resulting agent decision human-originated. The human delegated or approved the decision; the underlying choice may still have been generated by the agent.

Likewise, a human responding:

> “Yes.”

could represent careful agreement or casual acknowledgement. The transcript may not reveal how much thought the human actually put into it, so the system should avoid inventing a confidence score for unobservable mental effort.

What matters may be a richer set of observable relationships between humans, agents, proposals, decisions, tasks, and evidence.

## Context-dependent statements

Some human statements are not meaningful in isolation.

For example:

> “Yes, but do step 2 before step 1.”

requires the surrounding proposal to understand what “step 1” and “step 2” mean. It may also establish only the ordering change, rather than endorsing every other part of the agent's proposal.

The system therefore needs to preserve enough contextual evidence to interpret statements correctly. If that context is necessary to establish meaning, its integrity matters too; it should not be possible to keep a valid human message while substituting different surrounding context.

For important ambiguous cases, an agent may occasionally ask for explicit confirmation, such as:

> “Can I quote you on that?”

This should be exceptional rather than routine, otherwise users may begin reflexively approving provenance prompts and destroy the value of the signal.

## Extensibility and exhaustiveness

I do not want the correctness of the system to depend on us having invented an exhaustive taxonomy of every possible form of human or agent provenance.

We may initially identify concepts such as assertion, approval, selection, rejection, clarification, delegation, confirmation, preference, prohibition, or others. There may still be important cases we have not anticipated.

If the foundational schema depends on a closed enumeration of all meaningful interaction or intent types, discovering a new case later could require schema changes or reinterpretation of existing records.

I would therefore prefer:

1. a small set of concepts that we have good reason to believe are fundamental and stable;
2. extensible domain-specific semantic categories;
3. derived interpretations and policy judgments that can be corrected as our understanding improves.

Mature provenance systems and standards are particularly relevant here. I would place more confidence in an established general model and its extension mechanisms than in a taxonomy invented specifically for this project, unless there is a strong reason to believe that taxonomy is exhaustive.

W3C PROV seems especially relevant. I want to understand:

- what foundational primitives it standardizes;
- how it represents domain-specific semantics that were not anticipated by the core model;
- whether it relies on closed taxonomies or extension/specialization mechanisms;
- whether we can directly adopt or specialize it rather than creating a parallel foundational model;
- which parts of this problem it already models well;
- which parts—especially the normative distinction between meaningful human intent and agent discretion—still require domain-specific semantics.

The underlying representation should remain correct even if our current semantic vocabulary later proves incomplete.

## Agent information

Agent decisions should remain freely reconsiderable by later agents unless independently constrained by human intent, compatibility requirements, safety requirements, or other external constraints.

This includes implementation choices, architectural decisions, interpretations, assumptions, and other decisions that were not meaningfully fixed by a human.

A future agent should not preserve an earlier agent's decision merely because it is historical.

Agent rationale may still be useful, but the system should distinguish between:

- rationale explicitly stated at the time of the decision;
- rationale reconstructed later from historical evidence;
- a plausible retrospective explanation generated by an agent.

Reconstructed rationale should not silently become evidence of why the original decision was actually made.

## Task-local provenance

The system should preserve provenance at the level of individual tasks as well as long-lived project decisions.

For example, if I say:

> “There's this bug, XYZ. Fix that.”

the implementing agent may introduce many implementation decisions while solving it.

A reviewer should later be able to distinguish something like:

> The human reported this bug and requested that it be fixed. The implementation strategy, data structures, architecture, tests, and other choices were introduced by the agent unless separately constrained.

This temporal provenance is useful even when none of those implementation decisions become durable project-level intent.

## Evidence versus interpretation

The system will probably need agents to classify, normalize, summarize, or otherwise interpret source evidence.

Those interpretations must remain distinct from the evidence itself.

The design should distinguish between:

- historical evidence of what actually occurred;
- agent-created semantic classifications of that evidence;
- agent-created normalized statements or summaries;
- current policy judgments about what authority those statements should have.

Historical evidence should be immutable or otherwise tamper-evident.

Agent-created interpretations should be corrigible without erasing the original evidence or previous interpretations.

This also applies to claims such as idea origin. The system should avoid claiming more than the available evidence proves. It may be possible to say that an idea first appeared in the evidence currently available from an agent without proving that the idea never appeared in older human evidence that has not been found.

## Authentication, identity, and authority

If repository text itself is agent-writable, a field such as:

```yaml
actor: human
```

cannot by itself establish trusted human provenance.

If the design authenticates source events, it should explain how that trust is established.

Authentication and authority are separate concerns. In a project with multiple humans, it may matter not only that a statement came from a human, but which human made it and whether that person was authorized to establish durable intent for the relevant repository, component, or decision.

The model should therefore consider separately:

- source authentication;
- principal identity;
- authorization or authority scope;
- semantic interpretation of the statement.

## Storage and lifecycle

I want the design to consider whether provenance should live:

- in the repository;
- in remote storage;
- or in a hybrid model.

Different data may have different requirements, including:

- raw source evidence;
- normalized or structured provenance;
- agent-created interpretations;
- task-local provenance;
- durable project-level provenance;
- search indexes and other derived views.

The design should clearly distinguish the canonical source of truth from disposable indexes, caches, or retrieval infrastructure.

It should also define Git lifecycle semantics.

For example, if durable human intent is expressed while working on a feature branch that is later abandoned and never merged, should that intent disappear with the branch?

The system should explicitly define whether durable provenance is branch-scoped, repository/project-scoped, or maintained through some hybrid mechanism.

## Retrieval

The provenance system only helps if agents actually receive the relevant information while working.

The design should explain how provenance retrieval happens during coding, including:

- how relevant provenance is identified at task start;
- whether retrieval should be automatic rather than relying on an agent to remember to search;
- what happens when the implementation expands into code or components not known to be relevant initially;
- how provenance is ranked and packed into the agent's context without overwhelming it;
- how file, symbol, semantic, task, or historical links are used;
- how retrieval behaves after files are renamed, moved, split, merged, or reimplemented;
- how the system distinguishes “no matching provenance found” from “no provenance exists.”

Informing agents that the provenance system exists is easy. Making retrieval cheap, reliable, and difficult to accidentally skip is the harder problem.

## Links to code and refactoring

Direct links from provenance to files may become stale because files can be edited, renamed, moved, split, merged, or deleted.

The design should therefore evaluate whether provenance should attach directly to files or instead to something more stable, with file/symbol links serving only as retrieval hints.

Any proposed linkage model should explain how relationships survive ordinary refactoring and how uncertainty is represented when perfect recovery is impossible.

## Concurrency and identifiers

The repo uses Git worktrees and may have multiple agents working concurrently.

Provenance identifiers should therefore avoid centralized sequential allocation that can cause collisions or unnecessary merge conflicts.

The design should support concurrent creation, correction, and merging of provenance records without depending on a central mutable sequence.

## What the system should let an agent answer

A future agent should be able to determine:

- Was this behavior explicitly requested or constrained by a human?
- Was it selected, approved, delegated, clarified, rejected, or otherwise influenced by a human?
- Did the underlying idea originate with a human, an agent, mixed evidence, or is the origin unknown?
- Is this a durable human constraint, task-local instruction, advisory preference, agent decision, or historical behavior with unknown authority?
- What exactly did the human ask for during the task that produced this code?
- Which parts of the resulting implementation were introduced by the agent?
- What source evidence supports the current interpretation?
- Does that evidence depend on surrounding context?
- Which parts are immutable evidence versus corrigible interpretation?
- What happens if a later agent concludes an earlier classification or normalization was wrong?
- How should new semantic cases be represented if our initial vocabulary was incomplete?
- Are any closed enumerations being used without a convincing argument that they are exhaustive?
- Can W3C PROV or another mature provenance standard provide the stable foundational model?
- Which semantics genuinely need to be custom?
- How is source authenticity established?
- Which human principal made the statement, and what authority did they have?
- Where does canonical provenance live?
- How does it behave across branches, worktrees, abandoned changes, merges, and refactors?
- How will relevant provenance actually reach agents while they are coding?
- How should retrieval uncertainty and incomplete coverage be represented?
- Is existing behavior genuinely authoritative, or merely historical?

The system should preserve meaningful human intent without accidentally promoting agent-generated choices or interpretations into human authority.

It should also avoid requiring enough bookkeeping that agents stop using it.

I was working on the above system design and realized it overlaps with agent memory, provenance, software traceability, and related systems. I want to know whether existing memory systems, provenance systems, standards, or research already satisfy these requirements, or provide foundations we should reuse.
